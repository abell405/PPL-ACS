      Knowledge
      PA.I.A.K1

      Certification requirements, recent flight experience, and recordkeeping

      61.103 Eligibility — MEEEATC

      • Medical — 3rd class or higher (61.23)
      • Endorsements from an authorized instructor
      • Experience — 61.109 (Part 61) or Part 141 Appendix B
      • English — read, speak, write, and understand
      • Age — 17
      • Test — knowledge test (PAR) 70% or higher
      • Certificate — student pilot certificate

      Currency

      • 61.56 Flight review every 24 calendar months to act as PIC. A calendar month runs to midnight of the last day of that month.
      • 61.57 To carry passengers: 3 takeoffs and landings in the preceding 90 days, same category, class, and type (if type required). Full stop if tailwheel or at night.

      Category, class, type — 61.5 / 61.31

      • Category = Airplane · Class = Single Engine Land
      • Type rating (61.31) needed for a large aircraft (>12,500 lb, per 1.1), any turbojet, or any aircraft the Administrator specifies — e.g. Airbus A320.
      • Endorsements:
        • 61.31(e) Complex — flaps, retractable landing gear, and a controllable-pitch propeller (ground + flight)
        • 61.31(f) High performance — more than 200 hp per engine (ground + flight)
        • 61.31(g) Pressurized / high altitude — service ceiling or max operating altitude above 25,000' MSL (ground + flight)
        • 61.31(h) Type-specific training · 61.31(i) Tailwheel (flight only) · 61.31(j) Glider (ground + flight)
      Service ceiling — highest altitude where the airplane can still climb 50 fpm. P100i: 14,085' (POH 5-18).
      Absolute ceiling — where Vx and Vy meet. P100i: 16,400' (POH 5-18).

      61.51 Pilot logbooks

      Record the aeronautical experience used to meet recent flight experience and requirements for certificates, ratings, or flight reviews.

      PA.I.A.K2

      Privileges and limitations

      61.113 Private pilot privileges — PPSCRIPT

      • PIC and carry passengers
      • Pro rata — pay no less than your proportional share of operating expenses; never for compensation or hire
      • Salesman — aircraft salesman with 200 hours logged
      • Charity — 91.146, 500 hours
      • Rescue operation — reimbursement allowed
      • Incidental to business — the flight is incidental and no passengers or property carried for hire
      • Production flight test — 100 hours PIC in category/class
      • Towing — 61.69, 100 hours PIC in category/class

      Common purpose

      The pilot has their own reason for going to the destination, the pilot chooses the destination, and the passengers come from a defined group (not solicited publicly). See AC 61-142, Sharing Aircraft Operating Expenses.

      61.53 Self-grounding

      You may not act as a crewmember if you know or have reason to know of a medical condition that would make you unable to meet the requirements for your medical or to safely operate.

      Risk Management
      PA.I.A.R1

      Proficiency versus currency

      61.56 Flight review — every 24 calendar months

      • Minimum 1 hour ground and 1 hour flight
      • Satisfied by: a practical test, an armed forces proficiency check, or a phase of the FAA WINGS program
      • A CFI renewing their certificate need not do the 1 hour of ground

      61.57 — 3 TOLs in 90 days to carry passengers

      Currency = legal under the FARs. Proficiency = competent and safe. Being current does not make you proficient.
      PA.I.A.R2

      Flying unfamiliar aircraft or unfamiliar avionics

      An ASEL private pilot can legally fly any single-engine land airplane that doesn't need a type rating, but get training and aircraft familiarization first, before flying solo or with passengers. Reference: AC 90-109A.

      Checkride questions

      SCENARIOYou pass your checkride today and don't fly again for 80 days. Can you carry passengers? Would you?

      Legally yes: the checkride landings count toward 61.57's 3 TOLs in 90 days. But after 80 days off, you may not be proficient enough to fly passengers safely. Fly solo or with an instructor first.

      SCENARIOA friend's airline flight is cancelled and he asks you to fly him to his brother's wedding and split costs. Can you?

      Only if you have a common purpose, meaning you were already going to that destination for your own reason. Otherwise splitting costs is carrying a passenger for compensation.

      Name the 61.103 eligibility requirements.

      MEEEATC: Medical, Endorsements, Experience, English, Age 17, Test (70%+), student pilot Certificate.

      You are 45 with a 2nd class medical issued in March 2025. What privileges do you have in April 2026?

      Over 40, 2nd class privileges last 12 calendar months (through March 31, 2026). In April 2026 it is valid for 3rd class privileges, which last 24 calendar months (through March 31, 2027).

      What is the difference between a special issuance and a SODA?

      Special issuance is time-limited and re-evaluated, for conditions that can change. A SODA doesn't expire and is for static conditions.

      PA.I.B.K3a

      Flying with inoperative equipment

      91.213 — no MEL: MKART

      Without an approved MEL, you may take off with inoperative equipment only if it is not required by the:

      • KOEL
      • ADs
      • Regulations (91.205 or any other)
      • TCDS / type certificate

      Then it must be removed or deactivated and placarded "Inoperative," and the pilot must determine it isn't a hazard. Removal or deactivation is recorded per 43.9.

      PA.I.B.K3b

      Using an approved Minimum Equipment List (MEL)

      • MEL — equipment that can be inoperative while the aircraft stays airworthy. Specific to the tail number; developed by the operator and approved by the FSDO. Example: an inop coffee maker on an A320.
      PA.I.B.K3c

      Kinds of Operation Equipment List (KOEL)

      Equipment required to be installed and working for specific kinds of operations. The P100i is approved for day/night VFR, day/night IFR, non-icing (POH 2-6). POH 2-17 lists the G3X limitations, which give the required equipment for VFR and IFR.

      PA.I.B.K3d

      Required discrepancy records or placards

      Inop equipment allowed under 91.213(d) is placarded, and the records follow 43.11. Anyone approving or disapproving return to service logs: type of inspection, description of work, date, signature, certificate number, and certificate type.

      PA.I.B.K4

      Standard and special airworthiness certificates and their limitations

      • Standard — aircraft conforms to its type certificate and is in condition for safe operation. Valid while maintained, registered in the U.S., and with approved parts installed. Displayed to occupants and available to the FAA (91.203).
      • Special (experimental, light-sport, restricted, special flight permit, etc.) — can significantly limit operations: no persons or property for hire, plus possible geographic or time limits.

      Checkride questions

      SCENARIODuring preflight the left position light is out and you plan to return after sunset. Can you go?

      Position lights are required for night by 91.205(c) and 91.209, so you can't fly it at night. For a day-only flight, deactivate and placard it per 91.213 and confirm it isn't required by the KOEL, ADs, or TCDS.

      SCENARIOThe annual expired yesterday and the shop is at another airport. Options?

      You can't fly it on its own authority. Get a special flight permit (21.197 / 21.199) through the FSDO or a DAR to ferry it to the shop.

      The 100-hour was due at 600.0 tach. You overfly to 607.5 to reach the shop. When is the next one due?

      700.0. The 10-hour allowance only gets you to the inspection; the overflown time counts toward the next interval.

      Who is responsible for airworthiness: you or the owner?

      Both. The owner/operator maintains it in an airworthy condition (91.403); the PIC determines it's airworthy before each flight (91.7).

      What's the difference between an AD and an SAIB?

      An AD is regulatory and mandatory. An SAIB is informational with recommendations.

      Knowledge
      PA.I.C.K1

      Sources of weather data for flight planning

      • ForeFlight — our primary source. Pulls METARs, TAFs, PIREPs, AIRMETs/SIGMETs, radar, and winds aloft into one briefing, plus the official Flight Service briefing (Briefing tab). Its weather data comes from NOAA/NWS.
      • Aviation Weather Center — aviationweather.gov. The NWS site for aviation products: METARs, TAFs, PIREPs, AIRMETs/SIGMETs, GFA, progs, winds aloft. Good for checking a product at its source.
      • National Weather Service (part of NOAA) — the source behind both of the above.
      • Flight Service — 1-800-WX-BRIEF or 1800wxbrief.com. Briefings: standard, abbreviated, outlook (outlook when departure is 6+ hours away). In flight on 122.2 / 122.4 / 122.6 or via VOR/RCO frequencies.
      • FIS-B — not real-time. NEXRAD over ADS-B can be 15–20 minutes old, and the age shown may itself be off. Good for situational awareness; give storms extra room and don't use it to replace a preflight briefing.
      • Stormscope senses electrical discharge (lightning).
      PA.I.C.K2

      Acceptable weather products for preflight planning

      • Radar summary — precip type, intensity, coverage, movement. Updated hourly; valid only at observation time.
      • Low-level significant weather prog — flight category, turbulence, freezing level, fronts, pressure, precip. Issued 4×/day, valid 12 or 24 hours.
      Jet stream — narrow, fast current of air high in the atmosphere, generally west to east, where large temperature differences exist between air masses. Steers storms; strongest in winter. Coriolis effect — Earth's rotation deflects moving air, which makes winds aloft flow parallel to isobars.

      • From the Weather Prediction Center: analysis every 3 hours; 12- and 24-hr progs 4×/day; 36- and 48-hr progs 2×/day.

      Atmospheric composition and stability

      Composition: 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, 1% other gases.

      StableUnstable
      Stratiform cloudsCumuliform clouds
      Poor visibilityGood visibility

      • Stable air resists vertical development.

      PA.I.C.K3b

      Wind (wind shear, mountain wave, factors affecting wind)

      • Wind results from pressure differences.
      • Wind shear — sudden change in speed or direction over a short distance, vertical or horizontal. Found at fronts, low-level temperature inversions, CAT, and thunderstorms/microbursts.
      • Mountain wave — stable air (as little as 15 kt) flowing over a ridge sets up waves downwind; lenticular clouds; strong turbulence. Felt up to 700 NM downwind of the Rockies and 100 NM of the Appalachians (AIM 7-6-7).
      • Factors: pressure gradient force (closer isobars = stronger wind), Coriolis (parallel to isobars aloft), surface friction (angle across isobars at the surface).
      PA.I.C.K3c

      Temperature and heat exchange

      • Standard sea level: 29.92" Hg / 1013.2 mb, 15 °C; standard lapse 2 °C per 1,000'.
      Pressure altitude = (29.92 − altimeter setting) × 1,000 + field elevation ISA temp at altitude = 15 − (2 × altitude / 1,000)
      • Warm air holds more moisture; cooling air approaches its dew point.
      • Uneven heating drives all weather: temperature differences → pressure differences → wind.
      PA.I.C.K3d

      Moisture and precipitation

      • Humid air is less dense (water vapor is lighter than dry air), so engine and wing performance drop.
      • Freezing rain can cause rapid icing.

      Hydroplaning

      • Dynamic — water builds ahead of the tire and lifts it. Speed ≈ 8.6 × √tire pressure. P100i mains at 24 psi ≈ 42 kt.
      • Viscous — thin film the tire can't penetrate (smooth or rubber-coated surfaces).
      • Reverted rubber — locked wheel under heavy braking heats water into steam.

      Don't land fast on a wet runway, keep tires properly inflated, use aerodynamic braking. Grooved runways reduce but don't remove the risk.

      • Higher stall speed, less maneuverability

      PA.I.F.K3

      Aerodynamics

      Airfoil terms

      • Chord line — leading edge to trailing edge
      • Mean camber line — halfway between upper and lower surfaces
      • Relative wind — parallel and opposite the flight path
      • Angle of attack — between chord line and relative wind
      • Angle of incidence — between chord line and longitudinal axis (fixed)
      • Critical AOA — where airflow separates and the wing stalls. Constant for a given configuration, at any airspeed or weight.

      Lift

      L = ½ · ρ · V² · S · CL
      • ρ air density — can't control
      • S wing area — fixed, except Fowler flaps
      • V velocity — controllable; doubling speed quadruples lift
      • CL — rises with AOA up to the critical AOA
      • Newton's 3rd law: wing deflects air down, air pushes wing up. Bernoulli: faster flow over the upper surface → lower pressure (venturi).

      Drag

      • Parasite — drag from anything that doesn't help make lift. Increases with the square of airspeed. Three types:
        • Form — from the shape and frontal area of each part. Air can't follow the shape and separates into eddies behind it (landing gear, antennas, the cowling). Reduced by streamlining, like wheel pants and fairings.
        • Interference — where the airflow around two parts meets and mixes, such as the wing–fuselage joint or a strut meeting the wing. Together they make more drag than each part alone. Reduced with fillets that smooth the joint.
        • Skin friction — air rubbing against the airplane's surface in the thin boundary layer. Rivet heads, dirt, bugs, ice, and rough paint add to it. Reduced by smooth, clean surfaces and flush rivets.
      • Induced — byproduct of lift; decreases as speed increases
      • Where they cross = L/D max = best glide (76 KIAS)

      Wingtip vortices and wake turbulence

      • Strongest when heavy, clean, slow
      • Vortices sink and drift outward; light quartering tailwind is worst
      • Wait 2–3 minutes behind large aircraft. Land beyond their touchdown point; rotate before their rotation point (AIM 7-4)

      Ground effect (within about one wingspan)

      • Reduced induced drag can let you lift off before you can climb, or float on landing
      • Leaving it: more AOA needed, more induced drag, less stability with a nose-up moment, airspeed indication may read low

      Adverse yaw

      The rising (outside) wing makes more lift and more induced drag, yawing the nose away from the turn. P100i uses differential ailerons: up aileron 25°, down aileron 12.5°. Ranges are in the TCDS.

      Left-turning tendencies

      Torque, P-factor, spiraling slipstream, gyroscopic precession.

      Spins

      • Need: stalled + yaw (uncoordinated, usually a skid)
      • Danger zone: overshooting base-to-final with too much bank and rudder. Keep bank 30° or less in the pattern.
      • Recovery — REAP:
        • Rudder opposite the rotation (stop the yaw)
        • Elevator forward (break the stall, AOA below critical)
        • Ailerons neutral (avoid a spin the other way)
        • Power idle (reduce left-turning tendencies and rotation)
        Then neutralize rudder as rotation stops and recover from the dive.

      Thrust

      180 hp Lycoming, 76" fixed-pitch Sensenich prop. The blade is twisted because the tip moves faster than the root: lower AOA at the tip, higher at the root, for even thrust along the blade.

      Maneuvering speed and load factor

      • VO — max speed for a full, abrupt control deflection: the wing stalls before it overloads.
      • VO drops as weight drops (a lighter airplane reaches the critical AOA at less load).

      Calculating VO for your actual weight

      VO actual = VO max × √(actual weight ÷ max weight)
      • Example at 2,200 lb: 113 × √(2,200 ÷ 2,550) = 113 × √0.863 = 113 × 0.929 ≈ 105 KIAS
      • Check against the POH at 1,917 lb: 113 × √(1,917 ÷ 2,550) = 113 × 0.867 ≈ 98 KIAS, which matches the published value
      • Rule of thumb: reduce VO about 1% for every 2% below max gross weight
      • Load factor = lift ÷ weight (G). P100i normal category: +3.8 / −1.5 G, no inverted maneuvers (POH 2-6).
      • Stall speed rises with the square root of load factor (e.g. 60° bank = 2 G → about 41% higher stall speed).

      Stability

      • Static — initial tendency after a disturbance: positive (returns), neutral (stays), negative (diverges)
      • Dynamic — what happens over time: damped, undamped, or divergent oscillations. Needs positive static stability first.
      • Longitudinal — CG / horizontal tail · Lateral — dihedral · Directional — vertical tail

      Checkride questions

      SCENARIOField elevation 1,000', altimeter 29.82, temp 35 °C. What's the pressure altitude, and what does the heat mean?

      PA = (29.92 − 29.82) × 1,000 + 1,000 = 1,100'. ISA at 1,100' ≈ 13 °C, so it's 22 °C hotter than standard; density altitude is much higher (roughly 3,700'). Expect longer takeoff and weaker climb.

      SCENARIOYou're 30 lb over max gross with full fuel and three adults. Options?

      Reduce fuel (if reserves still work), leave baggage, or leave a passenger. Then recheck the CG at takeoff and landing.

      Why is VO lower when the airplane is light?

      Less weight means less lift needed, so a lower AOA. An abrupt deflection can then reach a high load before the wing stalls; a lower speed keeps the stall ahead of the limit load.

      Forward vs. aft CG: which stalls at a higher speed?

      Forward. More tail-down force means the wing flies at a higher AOA.

      What conditions make wake turbulence strongest?

      Heavy, clean, slow.

      Mixture

      • Best economy: lean to peak EGT on the first cylinder to peak
      • Best power: peak, then enrich 100 °F rich of peak (POH 4-18)

      Fuel injection

      • The servo meters fuel in proportion to airflow; the flow divider sends it to each cylinder's nozzle.

      Hyperventilation

      • Cause: breathing too fast or deep (stress, anxiety), too much CO₂ lost
      • Symptoms: visual impairment, dizziness, tingling, hot/cold sensations, muscle spasms, unconsciousness. Similar to hypoxia.
      • Treatment: slow the breathing rate, breathe into a bag, talk aloud
      PA.I.H.K1c

      Middle ear and sinus problems

      • Ear: the Eustachian tube equalizes the middle ear. Climbs vent easily; descents are harder because the tube is pressed shut.
      • Clear by swallowing, yawning, chewing, or Valsalva (pinch nose, close mouth, blow gently).
      • A cold or infection may make it impossible to clear and can damage the eardrum. Ask your AME before using nasal sprays.
      • Sinus block: usually in descent; severe pain above the eyes or in the cheeks/upper teeth, possibly bloody mucus. Slow the descent. Don't fly with congestion; see a doctor if it doesn't clear after landing.
      PA.I.H.K1d

      Spatial disorientation and illusions

      Orientation comes from the vestibular (inner ear semicircular canals), somatosensory (seat of the pants), and visual systems. Vision is the most reliable.

      ICE FLAGS

      InversionAbrupt climb-to-level feels like tumbling backward; pilot pushes nose low.
      CoriolisHead movement in a prolonged turn sets fluid moving in another canal: strong tumbling sensation. Keep head movements minimal.
      ElevatorUpdraft feels like a climb (pilot pushes over); downdraft the reverse.
      False horizonSloping clouds or ground lights mistaken for the horizon.
      LeansAfter a prolonged turn, leveling feels like a turn the other way; pilot banks back.
      AutokinesisA stationary light stared at seems to move.
      Graveyard spiralPilot feels level in a descending turn, pulls back, and tightens the spiral.
      SomatogravicRapid acceleration feels like a nose-up pitch (pilot pushes down); deceleration the reverse.

      Treatment: trust your instruments.

      PA.I.H.K1e

      Motion sickness

      • Conflicting signals to the brain. Usually fades after a few lessons.
      • Symptoms: discomfort, nausea, dizziness, paleness, sweating, vomiting.
      • Fix: open vents, look outside, limit head movement, start with short flights in smooth air. Avoid Dramamine-type drugs (drowsiness).
      PA.I.H.K1f

      Carbon monoxide poisoning

      • Colorless and odorless. Binds to hemoglobin about 200× more readily than oxygen (hypemic hypoxia). Can take up to 48 hours to clear.
      • Usually from an exhaust leak into the heater muff. Smell exhaust → assume CO.
      • Symptoms: headache, blurred vision, dizziness, drowsiness, loss of muscle power.
      • Action: heat/defrost off, fresh air vents open, oxygen if available, land as soon as possible.
      PA.I.H.K1g

      Stress

      Acute — short-term reaction to a specific event (fight or flight). Chronic — ongoing overload; may need a physician. AIM 8-1-1.

      PA.I.H.K1h

      Fatigue

      General loss of alertness and performance. Acute — usually fixed with a good night's sleep. Chronic — deeper causes (sleep apnea, insomnia) that rest doesn't fix; see a physician. AIM 8-1-1, AC 117-3.

      PA.I.H.K1i

      Dehydration and nutrition

      • Rule of thumb: drink about half your body weight (lb) in ounces daily. Thirsty means already dehydrated.
      • Coffee and alcohol are diuretics.
      • Eat regularly to keep energy, focus, and judgment.
      PA.I.H.K1j

      Hypothermia

      • Body loses heat faster than it makes it (cold air or water). 95 °F or below → medical help.
      • Symptoms: shivering, slurred speech, slow shallow breathing, weak pulse, clumsiness, drowsiness, confusion, unconsciousness.
      • Prevent with proper clothing and survival gear; treat by warming gradually.
